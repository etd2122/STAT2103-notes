R squared and correlation

What is \(R^{2}\)? Consider the following example data set.

plot(x, y, xlim=c(0.5, 5.5), ylim=c(-5, 10))
abline(model1, lwd=3)
legend("bottomright", legend=c("least squares \n regression line"), 
       lty=1, lwd=2, bty='n', cex=0.8)

The cor( ) function gives the sample correlation between a specified set of x and y values.

cor(x,y)
## [1] 0.8332724

This is a relatively large correlation. For a relationship between a single predictor variable x and response y, the \(R^{2}\) value can be obtained by squaring the correlation value.

cor(x,y)^2
## [1] 0.6943429

An \(R^{2}\) value of 0.7 means that in the fitted linear model, 70% of the variation in the observed y values is explained by the difference in x values.

Another way to calculate the \(R^{2}\) value is by calculating the sum of squared residuals from the fitted model, dividing by the sum of squared deviations of the y variable from its mean, and subtracting that ratio from 1.

# amount of y variation not explained by the linear model
sum(model1$residuals^2)
## [1] 41.93764
# total variation in y
sum((y-mean(y))^2) 
## [1] 137.2049
# percentage of y variation not explained by the model
sum(model1$residuals^2)/sum((y-mean(y))^2) 
## [1] 0.3056571
# percentage of y variation that IS explained by the model
1-sum(model1$residuals^2)/sum((y-mean(y))^2)
## [1] 0.6943429

It is the value labeled ``Multiple R-squared” in the second-to-last line.

An \(R^{2}\) value of 0.7 is relatively high. What would a model with a lower value look like?

plot(x2, y2, xlim=c(0.5, 5.5), ylim=c(-10, 15), pch=16)
abline(model2, lwd=3)
legend("topleft", legend=c("least squares \n regression line"), 
       lty=1, lwd=2, bty='n',cex=0.8)

For this new data set, the correlation is lower:

We can plot the two data sets side by side to see how they differ visually:

par(mfrow=c(1,2))
plot(x,y, main="higher correlation", 
     xlim=c(0.5, 5.5), ylim=c(-10,15), pch=20)
abline(model1)
plot(x2,y2, xlab="x", ylab="y", main = "lower correlation", 
     xlim=c(0.5, 5.5), ylim=c(-10,15), pch=20)
abline(model2)

When displayed over the same range of x- and y-axis values, it is clear that the data points are more spread out around the line in the second example, with the lower \(R^{2}\) value.

This method of calculating \(R^{2}\) and the correlation between x and y specifically measures the strength of linear associations between the two variables.

In both of the previous examples, the association between x and y was linear, but the strength of the association differed.

What happens when the association between x and y is NOT linear?

plot(x3, y3, xlim=c(0.5, 5.5), ylim=c(-5, 10),
     xlab="x", ylab="y")
abline(model3, lwd=3)
legend("topleft", legend=c("least squares \n regression line"),
       lty=1, lwd=2, bty='n')

Visually, there is clearly an association between x and y, and the association appears to be fairly strong.

But the least squares regression line does not capture that relationship, because the association is not linear.

The correlation between x and y is low:

cor(x3, y3)
## [1] -0.2076594

And the \(R^{2}\) value is close to zero:

summary(model3)
## 
## Call:
## lm(formula = y3 ~ x3)

## Residuals:

A low \(R^{2}\) can occur in scenarios where there is a weak linear association between predictor and response, and also in scenarios where the association between predictor and response is NON-linear.
